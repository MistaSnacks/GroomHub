Professional cat grooming is a service that includes bathing, brushing, nail trimming, ear cleaning, and optional haircuts, typically costing $50 to $150 per session. While cats are famously self-grooming, many benefit from professional care, especially long-haired breeds, senior cats, and cats with skin conditions. After 15 years of grooming cats in my salon, I can tell you that regular professional cat grooming makes a real difference in a cat's comfort and health. Here's what to expect from start to finish.

## Do Cats Really Need Professional Grooming?

The biggest mistake cat owners make is assuming their cat handles all its own grooming. Short-haired cats generally manage their own coats with regular brushing at home, but professional cat grooming becomes necessary in these situations:

- **Long-haired breeds** like Persians, Maine Coons, Ragdolls, and Himalayans are prone to matting that's painful and difficult to remove at home.
- **Senior cats** often lose the flexibility to groom themselves properly, leading to mats, long nails, and skin issues.
- **Overweight cats** may not be able to reach certain areas of their body.
- **Cats with skin conditions** benefit from medicated baths and professional attention.
- **Indoor cats** with minimal activity tend to develop longer nails faster, since they don't wear them down on outdoor surfaces.

Professional grooming also helps prevent hairballs, keeps nails at a safe length, and lets a trained eye catch skin problems early. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/) recommends regular grooming as part of routine cat care. In my salon, I see at least one or two cats a month where I'm the first person to notice a lump, a skin irritation, or an ear infection the owner didn't know about.

## What's Included in a Cat Grooming Session

A standard professional cat grooming session takes 1 to 2 hours and includes these steps:

1. **Assessment.** The groomer examines your cat's coat condition, skin, nails, and ears before starting.
2. **Bath.** A gentle bath with cat-safe shampoo. Some groomers use hypoallergenic or medicated formulas depending on your cat's needs.
3. **Blow drying.** Most groomers use low-heat dryers. Some cats tolerate this well; others need extra patience and breaks.
4. **Brushing and dematting.** Loose fur is removed and any mats are carefully worked out or shaved if too tight.
5. **Nail trimming.** Nails are clipped to a safe length.
6. **Ear cleaning.** Ears are gently cleaned to remove wax buildup.
7. **Sanitary trim.** The fur around the rear is trimmed for hygiene.
8. **Haircut (optional).** Full haircuts, including lion cuts, are available for cats with severe matting or for owners who prefer a lower-maintenance coat.

Not every cat needs every step. After years of grooming cats, I've learned that flexibility matters more than checking every box. If a cat is too stressed for a full bath, I'll do a waterless shampoo and focus on the dematting and nail trim instead. A partial groom that keeps the cat calm is always better than a complete groom that traumatizes them.

## Cat Grooming vs. Dog Grooming: Key Differences

Cat grooming is not just "dog grooming but smaller." The two require very different skills, and not every dog groomer is equipped to work with cats. Here's what sets cat grooming apart:

- **Thinner skin.** Cats have significantly thinner, more delicate skin than dogs. Clipper burns and nicks happen more easily if the groomer isn't trained in feline techniques. I use a lighter touch and different blade guards for cats than I would for any dog.
- **Different temperament.** Dogs can usually be coached or distracted during grooming. Cats either cooperate or they don't, and pushing a cat past its threshold can result in bites, scratches, and a cat that will never willingly go to a groomer again.
- **Retractable claws.** Cats can extend and retract their claws instantly, which means restraint and handling need to account for sudden scratching. This is second nature for experienced cat groomers, but it catches dog-only groomers off guard.
- **Restraint techniques.** Dogs are typically secured with a grooming loop. Cats require a completely different approach: gentle scruffing, towel wrapping (the "purrito" method), or simply working with a calm, confident hand. Force-based restraint almost always backfires.
- **Shorter appointment times.** Most cats can only tolerate 30 to 90 minutes of grooming before stress builds. Dog appointments often run 2 to 3 hours. A good cat groomer works efficiently and knows when to call it.
- **Noise sensitivity.** Cats are more reactive to the sound of clippers and dryers than most dogs. I keep my cat grooming area separate and quieter than the dog section for this reason.

If your current groomer only works with dogs, look specifically for a groomer with feline experience or NCGI ([National Cat Groomers Institute](https://nationalcatgroomers.com/)) certification.

## Breed-Specific Cat Grooming Needs

Different breeds have very different grooming requirements. Here's what I see most often in my salon.

### Persian Cats

Persians are one of the most grooming-intensive breeds. Their long, dense coats mat easily, especially around the armpits, belly, and behind the ears. In my experience, Persians need professional grooming every 4 to 6 weeks to stay comfortable.

Beyond coat care, Persians need regular eye cleaning. Their flat facial structure causes tear staining, and dried discharge builds up quickly around the eyes. I clean this gently with a warm, damp cloth during every appointment. At home, owners should wipe the eye area daily.

Many Persian owners opt for a lion cut during summer months to reduce matting and keep their cat cool. This is a perfectly valid choice and one I recommend for Persians whose coats have gotten out of control between appointments.

### Maine Coons

Maine Coons have a thick double coat with a dense undercoat that sheds heavily, especially in spring and fall. The biggest challenge with Maine Coons is undercoat management. If the undercoat isn't properly thinned and brushed out, it packs down against the skin and forms pelted mats that are painful to remove.

I recommend professional grooming every 6 to 8 weeks for Maine Coons, with regular brushing at home between visits. A good slicker brush and a wide-tooth comb are essential. Pay special attention to the "pantaloons" (the longer fur on the hind legs) and the ruff around the neck, both of which mat quickly.

Maine Coons are generally more tolerant of grooming than other breeds, which helps. Most of the Maine Coons I work with are calm and cooperative, making them a good candidate for a full grooming session.

### Ragdolls

Ragdolls have a silky, semi-long coat that's less prone to matting than a Persian's but still needs regular attention. The texture is finer and softer, which means mats can sneak up on owners who assume the coat is "easy."

Professional grooming every 6 to 8 weeks is usually sufficient for Ragdolls. The focus should be on keeping the coat tangle-free, trimming the sanitary area, and managing shedding. Ragdolls tend to be laid-back during grooming sessions, living up to their name.

### Siamese and Short-Haired Breeds

Short-haired breeds like the Siamese, British Shorthair, and Domestic Shorthair are the lowest-maintenance cats when it comes to grooming. Their coats rarely mat, and they handle self-grooming well.

That said, these cats still benefit from professional nail trims every 4 to 6 weeks and a bath 2 to 4 times per year to reduce shedding and dander. I also see short-haired cats for sanitary trims and ear cleaning when owners notice buildup.

The main value of professional grooming for short-haired cats is the health check. I've caught skin conditions, ear mites, and overgrown nails on short-haired cats whose owners thought they didn't need grooming at all.

### Sphynx and Hairless Breeds

Sphynx cats don't have fur, but they actually need more frequent bathing than most cats. Without fur to absorb natural skin oils, Sphynx cats develop a greasy buildup on their skin that can clog pores and cause acne. They also accumulate debris in their ear canals and between their toes faster than coated breeds.

I recommend bathing a Sphynx every 1 to 2 weeks. This can be done at home or professionally. The bath should use a gentle, pH-balanced cat shampoo. Avoid anything with harsh detergents, as Sphynx skin is sensitive.

Professional grooming for Sphynx cats focuses on thorough bathing, ear cleaning (these cats produce a lot of ear wax), nail trimming, and checking the skin folds for irritation. It's a shorter appointment since there's no coat work, but it's just as important.

## Signs Your Cat Needs Professional Grooming

Cats are subtle about discomfort. By the time you notice a problem, it may have been building for weeks. Watch for these signs that it's time to book an appointment:

- **Visible mats or tangles.** If you can see or feel clumps in the coat, especially near the belly, armpits, or behind the ears, a professional needs to address them. Pulling mats at home risks tearing the skin.
- **Excessive shedding.** If your cat is leaving fur on every surface despite regular brushing, a professional deshedding treatment can remove the loose undercoat that home brushing misses.
- **Greasy or dull coat.** A healthy coat should look clean and have some sheen. A greasy, clumpy, or dull coat often means the cat isn't grooming itself well or has a skin issue.
- **Overgrown or curling nails.** Cat nails that start curling back toward the paw pad can grow into the pad and cause pain and infection. This is especially common in senior cats and indoor cats.
- **Dirty ears.** Brown or black buildup in the ears, head shaking, or scratching at the ears all signal that professional cleaning is needed (and possibly a vet visit to rule out mites or infection).
- **Staining around the rear.** Fecal matter stuck to the fur around the tail area is both a hygiene and comfort issue. A sanitary trim solves this.
- **Dandruff or flaky skin.** This can indicate dry skin, poor nutrition, or the beginning of a dermatological issue that a groomer can spot during a bath.

## Cat Grooming Cost Breakdown

Cat grooming costs vary by service type, coat length, and your location. Here's what you can expect to pay in 2026:

| Service | Price Range |
|---|---|
| Bath and blow dry | $50 - $80 |
| Full groom (bath, brush, nail trim, ear clean, sanitary trim) | $70 - $120 |
| Lion cut | $80 - $150 |
| Nail trim only | $15 - $25 |
| Dematting (added to any service) | $20 - $50 extra |
| Sanitary trim only | $15 - $25 |
| Ear cleaning only | $10 - $15 |

A few things affect the final price. Severely matted cats will cost more because dematting is time-intensive and requires careful work to avoid injuring the skin. Cats that are aggressive or extremely fearful may incur an additional handling fee ($10 to $30) because they require more time, extra safety precautions, and sometimes a second groomer to assist.

Long-haired breeds generally cost more than short-haired cats for any service due to the extra coat work involved.

Tipping your cat groomer is appreciated but not required. If you're happy with the service, 15 to 20 percent is standard. Cat grooming is physically and mentally demanding work, and a good cat groomer has spent years developing the patience and skill the job requires.

## How Often Does a Cat Need Professional Grooming?

How often a cat needs professional grooming depends on coat type, age, and health. Here are my recommendations based on what I've seen work over 15 years:

- **Long-haired cats (Persian, Himalayan, some Maine Coons):** Every 4 to 6 weeks. These coats mat fast, and waiting longer means more dematting (which means more stress and higher cost).
- **Medium-haired cats (Maine Coon, Ragdoll, Birman):** Every 6 to 8 weeks. These coats are more forgiving, but regular maintenance prevents problems from building up.
- **Short-haired cats (Siamese, Domestic Shorthair, British Shorthair):** Nail trim every 4 to 6 weeks. A professional bath 2 to 4 times per year.
- **Hairless cats (Sphynx, Peterbald):** Bath every 1 to 2 weeks. Ear cleaning and nail trim every 2 to 4 weeks.
- **Senior cats (any coat type):** Every 4 to 6 weeks, since they can't self-groom as effectively. Senior cats benefit from more frequent check-ins.
- **Cats with skin issues:** Per your vet's recommendation, often monthly.

Consistency matters more than frequency. A cat that comes in on a regular schedule is easier to groom, less stressed, and costs less per visit because there's less buildup to deal with.

## Finding a Cat-Friendly Groomer

Cat grooming requires specialized skills that not all dog groomers have. The [National Cat Groomers Institute (NCGI)](https://nationalcatgroomers.com/) is the primary certification body for feline groomers in the United States. When choosing a cat groomer, look for:

- NCGI certification or feline-specific training.
- Separate cat areas away from dogs, or cat-only salons.
- A calm, patient approach with willingness to take breaks.
- Experience handling cats that are anxious or resistant to grooming.

## How to Prepare Your Cat for Grooming

Preparation makes a real difference in how a grooming session goes. Here's what I recommend to my clients.

### 2 to 3 Weeks Before the Appointment

Start getting your cat comfortable with being handled. Practice touching their paws (gently pressing the pads to extend the nails), looking in their ears, and lifting their tail for a sanitary area check. Reward calm behavior with treats. Even a few minutes of handling practice each day makes the groomer's job significantly easier.

If your cat has never been in a carrier, start carrier training now. Leave the carrier out with the door open, put treats inside, and let your cat explore it voluntarily. A cat that panics in the carrier arrives at the salon already stressed, which makes everything harder.

### The Day Before

Brush your cat gently if they'll allow it. This isn't about getting the coat perfect. It's about removing the worst of the loose fur so the groomer can focus on the deeper work. Don't try to cut out mats yourself with scissors. I've seen too many cats come in with skin lacerations from well-meaning owners who tried to remove mats at home.

### Day of the Appointment

- Bring them in a secure, well-ventilated carrier. Hard-sided carriers with a top-opening door are ideal because the groomer can lift the cat out gently rather than pulling them through a front door.
- Avoid feeding them right before the appointment to prevent car sickness.
- Bring any relevant health information, especially if your cat is on medication or has skin allergies.
- Let the groomer know about any behavioral concerns (biting, scratching, extreme fear).
- If your cat has a favorite blanket or toy, bring it along. Familiar scents help reduce anxiety.

A good groomer will work at your cat's pace and won't force the process. If your cat is too stressed, a partial groom is better than a traumatic full session.

## A Note on Sedation

Sedation for cat grooming should only be used as a last resort and must be prescribed by a veterinarian, as the [AVMA](https://www.avma.org/) advises consulting your vet before any sedation procedure. Light sedation carries risks including respiratory depression and adverse reactions, particularly in older cats. Most experienced cat groomers, especially those certified by the NCGI, can handle nervous cats without sedation by using gentle restraint techniques, frequent breaks, and a calm environment.

In my salon, I very rarely recommend sedation. In 15 years, I've needed it for maybe a handful of cats. The key is building trust over multiple visits. A cat that was difficult the first time often settles down by the third or fourth appointment once it learns that the process isn't going to hurt.

Talk to your vet and your groomer before considering this option.

## FAQ

**Should cats be professionally groomed?**
Yes, many cats benefit from professional grooming. Long-haired breeds like Persians and Maine Coons need professional grooming every 4 to 8 weeks to prevent painful matting. Senior and overweight cats often cannot groom themselves effectively. Short-haired cats typically need only nail trims every 4 to 6 weeks and a bath 2 to 4 times per year.

**Do cats feel better after being groomed?**
Most cats are calmer and more comfortable after matting is removed, nails are trimmed, and excess fur is cleared. The grooming process itself can be stressful, but the results improve their comfort and hygiene. I've had clients tell me their cat was noticeably more active and playful after a dematting session because the pulling and pressure from the mats was gone.

**Is it safe to sedate a cat for grooming?**
Sedation is generally safe when prescribed by a veterinarian, but it carries risks including respiratory depression and should only be used as a last resort. Most NCGI-certified cat groomers can handle nervous cats without sedation using gentle techniques and frequent breaks.

**How do I find a cat groomer near me?**
Look for groomers with NCGI certification or feline-specific training, separate cat grooming areas away from dogs, and positive reviews from other cat owners. On GroomLocal, you can [search for cat groomers](/cat-grooming) in your area and filter by services offered.

**Can I groom my cat at home instead of going to a professional?**
You can handle basic maintenance at home: regular brushing, nail trims (if your cat cooperates), and ear checks. However, professional groomers have the tools, training, and experience to handle baths, dematting, lion cuts, and skin assessments that are difficult or risky to do at home. For long-haired breeds and senior cats, a combination of home brushing and professional grooming works best.

**What age should a cat start professional grooming?**
I recommend starting kittens around 8 to 12 weeks old with a short introductory visit. The goal isn't a full groom. It's getting the kitten used to being handled, hearing the dryer, and being on a grooming table. Cats that start young are dramatically easier to groom as adults. If your adult cat has never been groomed, a patient intro visit is still a good first step.

**Why does cat grooming cost more than a nail trim at the vet?**
A veterinary nail trim takes a few minutes and uses basic restraint. A full cat grooming session involves bathing, drying, coat work, nail trimming, ear cleaning, and often 30 to 90 minutes of careful, skilled handling. The price reflects the groomer's time, specialized training, and the equipment and products used. Cat grooming also requires more patience and adaptability than dog grooming, since cats set the pace.
