Most dogs need professional grooming every 4 to 8 weeks. Short-coated breeds (Labs, Beagles) can go 8 to 12 weeks between visits, while long-coated and curly breeds (Poodles, Doodles) need grooming every 4 to 6 weeks. The exact schedule depends on coat type, activity level, and climate.

After 15 years of grooming in the Pacific Northwest, I can tell you that the single biggest factor in how often a dog needs professional grooming is whether the owner brushes at home. A Goldendoodle that gets brushed every other day can comfortably go 6 weeks between salon visits. The same dog with no home brushing? I'm seeing them every 3 to 4 weeks, and we're still dealing with mats.

## Professional Grooming Frequency by Coat Type

### Short-Coated Breeds (Labs, Beagles, Boxers, Pugs)

Short-coated breeds need professional grooming every 8 to 12 weeks. Weekly brushing at home, combined with regular nail trims and ear cleaning, is sufficient between visits. Short-coated breeds still shed seasonally, and a professional de-shedding treatment can reduce shedding by up to 80%, according to grooming industry estimates.

### Double-Coated Breeds (Huskies, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds)

Double-coated breeds need professional grooming every 6 to 8 weeks. These breeds have a dense undercoat that sheds heavily twice a year (spring and fall), and de-shedding treatments during these periods help manage loose fur. Brush at home 3 to 4 times per week to prevent matting in the undercoat.

Never shave a double-coated breed. The undercoat acts as insulation in both hot and cold weather, and it [doesn't grow back properly](https://www.aspca.org/) if removed.

### Long-Coated and Curly Breeds (Poodles, Doodles, Shih Tzus)

Long-coated and curly breeds require the most frequent professional grooming: every 4 to 6 weeks. Their coats grow continuously and mat quickly, so regular trims and thorough brushing are essential to prevent painful tangles. Daily brushing at home is ideal. If you have a Goldendoodle, check out our [Goldendoodle grooming guide](/blog/goldendoodle-grooming-guide) for breed-specific tips.

In my salon, the most common mistake I see is owners waiting too long between appointments for their curly-coated dogs. By the time they come in at 10 or 12 weeks, the matting is so severe that a short shave-down is the only humane option. That's frustrating for everyone, especially the dog.

### Wire-Coated Breeds (Schnauzers, Terriers, Wirehaired Pointers)

Wire-coated breeds need professional grooming every 6 to 8 weeks. Hand-stripping, rather than clipping, is the preferred method to maintain the coat's proper wiry texture and color. Not all groomers offer hand-stripping, so confirm availability when booking. Between appointments, brush 2 to 3 times per week.

<Callout>
Pro tip: Brush your dog at home between grooming appointments. Even 5 minutes a day can prevent matting and make professional grooming sessions shorter and less stressful.
</Callout>

## Grooming Schedule by Life Stage

Your dog's grooming needs change significantly as they age. A schedule that works for a two-year-old dog won't be right for a puppy or a senior.

### Puppies (8 Weeks to 1 Year)

The first professional groom should happen between 8 and 16 weeks of age, depending on the breed and vaccination schedule. This initial visit is about socialization, not styling. A good groomer will introduce your puppy to the table, the dryer, nail clippers, and gentle handling in a low-pressure session that lasts 15 to 30 minutes.

From there, schedule follow-up visits every 3 to 4 weeks until the puppy is about 6 months old. Yes, that's more frequent than what most adult dogs need. But these short, positive sessions build the foundation for a lifetime of stress-free grooming. Skipping this step is something I see owners regret later, when their 50-pound adolescent dog panics at the sound of clippers.

After 6 months, you can transition to the standard schedule for your dog's coat type. For a detailed breakdown of what to expect at each stage, see our [puppy first grooming guide](/blog/puppy-first-grooming-guide).

<Callout>
Puppy tip: Start handling your puppy's paws, ears, and muzzle at home from the first week. This makes every future grooming appointment easier for your dog and your groomer.
</Callout>

### Adult Dogs (1 to 7 Years)

Adult dogs follow the standard grooming frequency for their coat type (see the breed table above). This is the maintenance phase where consistency matters most. Pick a schedule and stick with it. Irregular grooming creates a cycle of matting, longer appointments, and a stressed-out dog.

In the Pacific Northwest, I adjust schedules seasonally. Fall and winter bring constant rain and mud, which means double-coated breeds especially benefit from more frequent de-shedding treatments. I usually recommend clients move from every 8 weeks to every 6 weeks from October through March. Our [PNW seasonal grooming guide](/blog/pnw-seasonal-dog-grooming-guide) covers this in more detail.

### Senior Dogs (7+ Years)

Senior dogs often need more frequent grooming, but the sessions should be gentler and shorter. Older dogs may develop thinner skin, arthritis that makes standing difficult, or skin conditions that need monitoring. I break longer grooming sessions into two shorter visits when I'm working with a senior dog that tires easily.

Key adjustments for senior grooming:

- **More frequent nail trims.** Senior dogs are often less active, so their nails grow faster without natural wear.
- **Gentler brushing.** Thinner skin bruises more easily. Use a softer brush and lighter pressure.
- **Skin checks.** Older dogs are more prone to lumps, dry patches, and hot spots. A groomer who sees your dog regularly can catch changes early.
- **Shorter sessions.** If your senior dog can't comfortably stand for a full groom, ask your groomer about splitting the appointment across two visits.
- **Warm environment.** Senior dogs get cold more easily. Make sure the grooming area is warm and the dryer isn't set too high.

For more on adapting your grooming routine as your dog ages, including budget-friendly options, see our [senior dog grooming guide](/blog/senior-dog-grooming-budget-guide).

## How Often Should You Bathe Your Dog?

Most dogs should be bathed every 4 to 6 weeks. Bathing frequency is separate from overall grooming frequency. Over-bathing strips natural oils from the coat, leading to dry skin and irritation, according to the [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/grooming/).

Some guidelines by coat type:

- **Short-coated breeds:** Every 6 to 8 weeks, or when noticeably dirty or smelly.
- **Double-coated breeds:** Every 6 to 8 weeks. Use a de-shedding shampoo during heavy shedding periods.
- **Long and curly coats:** Every 3 to 4 weeks, especially if the coat picks up dirt and debris easily.
- **Dogs with skin conditions:** Follow your vet's recommendation. Some conditions require weekly medicated baths, while others benefit from less frequent washing.

If your dog swims regularly or spends a lot of time outdoors in the Pacific Northwest rain, you may need to bathe more often. Use a gentle, dog-specific shampoo and always dry thoroughly. For more on managing wet weather grooming, see our [winter grooming tips](/blog/winter-grooming-tips-pnw-dogs).

## How Often Should You Trim Your Dog's Nails?

Dogs need nail trims every 2 to 4 weeks. The "click test" is the simplest way to check: if you hear nails clicking on hard floors, they're overdue. Overgrown nails cause discomfort, change your dog's gait, and can contribute to long-term joint problems, per the [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/grooming/).

Dogs that walk frequently on concrete or asphalt may naturally wear their nails down and need less frequent trimming. Dogs that mostly walk on grass or soft ground, which is common in the Pacific Northwest, will need more regular trims.

If you're not comfortable trimming nails at home, most groomers offer walk-in nail trim services between full appointments.

## Signs Your Dog Is Overdue for Grooming

Even with a regular schedule, life gets busy and appointments slip. Here are the practical signs that your dog needs professional attention sooner rather than later:

- **Matting behind the ears, under the legs, or around the collar.** These friction zones mat first. If you can't run a comb smoothly through these areas, it's time to book.
- **Nails clicking on hard floors.** This means nails are too long and affecting your dog's posture.
- **A noticeable "doggy" smell.** A clean, healthy coat should not have a strong odor. Persistent smell usually means it's been too long since a proper bath and blow-dry.
- **Eye discharge or tear staining buildup.** This can lead to skin irritation around the eyes if left too long.
- **Excessive scratching or biting at the coat.** This could signal matting pulling at the skin, dry skin from missed brushing, or a skin condition that a groomer would catch.
- **Fur covering the eyes or blocking vision.** This is a safety issue, not just cosmetic. Dogs that can't see well become anxious and reactive.
- **Overgrown hair between paw pads.** This collects debris and can cause slipping on smooth surfaces. In the PNW, wet grass and mud get packed between the pads and create a breeding ground for bacteria.
- **Scooting or discomfort around the rear.** This may indicate sanitary areas need trimming, or it could be a vet issue. Either way, a groomer can help identify what's going on.

I'll be honest: by the time most of these signs are visible, the dog has been uncomfortable for a while. The best approach is to set a recurring appointment rather than waiting for problems to appear.

## At-Home Maintenance Between Appointments

At-home grooming between professional appointments is just as important as salon visits. A consistent weekly routine prevents matting, catches health issues early, and keeps grooming sessions shorter:

- **Brushing:** Match your brushing frequency to your dog's coat type (see the breed table above). Use the right tool: slicker brushes for long coats, bristle brushes for short coats, undercoat rakes for double coats.
- **Teeth:** Brush your dog's teeth several times per week. Dental health is one of the most overlooked aspects of grooming.
- **Ears:** Check weekly for redness, odor, or buildup. Clean with a vet-approved ear cleaner as needed.
- **Eyes:** Wipe away discharge with a damp cloth, especially for flat-faced breeds.
- **Paws:** Check between pads for debris, cracks, or irritation after walks.

## FAQ

**How often should dogs go to a groomer?**
Most dogs benefit from professional grooming every 4 to 8 weeks, depending on coat type. Long and curly coats need the most frequent visits (every 4 to 6 weeks), while short-coated breeds can go 8 to 12 weeks between appointments.

**How long can a dog go without grooming?**
Short-coated dogs can go 2 to 3 months without professional grooming if you maintain brushing and nail trims at home. Long-coated and curly breeds should not go longer than 6 to 8 weeks, as mats form quickly in these coat types and can become painful and lead to skin infections.

**How often should you bathe your dog?**
Every 4 to 6 weeks for most dogs. Over-bathing strips natural oils from the coat. Dogs that are frequently outdoors in wet or muddy conditions may need baths more often, but always use a gentle, dog-specific shampoo.

**How often should you trim your dog's nails?**
Every 2 to 4 weeks. If you hear nails clicking on the floor, they're too long. Regular trims prevent discomfort and long-term joint issues.

**Do puppies need grooming?**
Yes. Puppies should have their first grooming visit between 8 and 16 weeks old. These early sessions focus on socialization rather than styling, and they set the tone for how your dog handles grooming for the rest of their life. See our [puppy grooming guide](/blog/puppy-first-grooming-guide) for a full breakdown.

**Does the Pacific Northwest climate affect grooming frequency?**
Absolutely. The constant rain and mud in Oregon and Washington mean dogs get dirty faster, especially between October and March. Many PNW groomers (myself included) recommend shortening the gap between appointments by a couple of weeks during the wet season. Double-coated breeds in particular benefit from more frequent de-shedding during fall and [spring coat transitions](/blog/pnw-seasonal-dog-grooming-guide).

**What happens if I wait too long between grooming appointments?**
The most common consequence is matting, which pulls at the skin and causes discomfort. Severe mats restrict airflow to the skin, creating conditions for hot spots and infections. Overgrown nails change your dog's gait and can lead to joint strain over time. In most cases, a dog that's gone too long between grooms will need a shorter clip than the owner wanted, simply because there's no safe way to brush out severe matting without causing pain.
