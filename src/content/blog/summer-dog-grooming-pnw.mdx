The most common summer grooming question PNW dog owners ask is whether to shave their dog. The short answer: do not shave double-coated breeds, do trim single-coated breeds, and always do sanitary trims regardless of coat type. The longer answer involves understanding how dog coats actually work, what wildfire smoke does to your dog's eyes and ears, and why shaving a Husky in July is one of the fastest ways to get a sunburned, overheated dog.

This guide covers what actually works for Pacific Northwest summers, where heat is short but intense, smoke season runs July through September, and most dogs are double-coated breeds bred for cool, wet weather.

## Should You Shave Your Dog in Summer?

The answer depends on coat type. Here is the simple rule:

| Coat type | Shave? | What to do instead |
|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| Double-coated (Husky, GSD, Golden, Aussie, Sammy, Bernese) | NO | Deshed every 4 to 8 weeks |
| Single-coated (Poodle, Doodle, Maltese, Yorkie, Bichon) | OK to trim | Summer cut at 1/2 to 1 inch |
| Hairless (Xolo, Crested) | N/A | Sunscreen and shade |
| Light/thin coat (Pit, Whippet, white Boxer) | NO | Sunscreen and shade |

If your groomer refuses to shave your double-coated dog, that is a sign of a good groomer. Many salons require a release waiver for double-coat shave-downs because of how often it goes wrong.

### Why Shaving Double Coats Backfires

Double coats are not like a wool sweater you can just remove. They are a built-in climate control system with two layers that do different jobs:

- **The undercoat** is dense, downy, and insulating. It traps a layer of cool air close to the skin and keeps body heat from radiating in.
- **The guard coat** is the longer, coarser top layer. It deflects UV rays and rain and shields the skin from heat exposure.

Together, these layers keep dogs cooler than bare skin would. Thermal imaging studies have shown that shaved double-coated dogs run hotter than dogs with intact topcoats, not cooler.

There is also a coat-damage problem. After a shave-down, the undercoat regrows faster than the guard hairs and crowds them out. The coat often comes back patchy, woolly, faded in color, and sometimes never returns to normal. This is called post-clipping alopecia, and it is most common in arctic breeds and senior dogs.

Dr. Jerry Klein, the AKC's Chief Veterinary Officer, has been blunt about this: shaving a double coat "eliminates that insulating layer of fur, makes the dog susceptible to heat stroke, and can result in improper hair growth and follicle damage."

<Callout>
The myth: "He'll be cooler without all that fur."
The reality: dogs do not sweat through their skin. They cool through panting and through their paw pads. Removing fur does not give them an extra cooling pathway. It just exposes their skin to sun and removes their insulation.
</Callout>

### The Real Solution: Deshedding

What works for double-coated dogs is removing the *loose* undercoat while leaving the structural coat intact. A professional deshed includes:

- A high-velocity dryer to blow out loose undercoat
- An undercoat rake to lift packed dead hair
- A slicker brush to finish

Done every 4 to 8 weeks through spring and summer, this restores airflow without damaging the coat. PNW heavy-coat breeds especially benefit from a deshed before Memorial Day and again in late July.

## Cooling Cuts for Single-Coated Breeds

If you have a Doodle, Poodle, Bichon, or other single-coated breed, summer is when shorter cuts make sense. Common options:

- **Puppy cut**: 1 to 2 inches all over, body and legs uniform. Easy to maintain.
- **Summer cut**: 1/2 to 3/4 inch on the body, fuller on the face and legs. Common request for Doodles.
- **Kennel cut**: 1/2 inch all over. Lowest maintenance, longest stretch between grooms (8 to 10 weeks).
- **Sanitary trim only**: belly, paw pads, ear tips. Safe for any breed including double-coated dogs.

The AKC recommends keeping single-coated dogs at least 1 inch long even in summer to protect from UV exposure and to keep insects from reaching skin.

| Breed | Recommended summer length |
|-------|---------------------------|
| Goldendoodle | 1/2 to 3/4 inch body, 1 to 2 inches face/legs |
| Standard Poodle | 1/2 to 1 inch body, longer top knot |
| Maltese, Bichon | 1 to 1.5 inches |
| Shih Tzu | 1 to 2 inches (or full coat with daily brushing) |
| Wire-haired terriers | hand strip, do not clip |

For a deeper Doodle-specific cut breakdown, see our [Goldendoodle grooming guide](/blog/goldendoodle-grooming-guide).

<Callout>
Pro tip: Sanitary trims are the most underrated summer service. Trimming belly fur, paw pad fur, and the area around the genitals helps cooling without touching the protective topcoat. Ask for a sanitary trim even if your double-coated dog is keeping a full body coat.
</Callout>

## Sunburn: Which Dogs Are at Risk

Yes, dogs get sunburned. The risk is highest for:

- **Hairless breeds**: Xoloitzcuintli, Chinese Crested, American Hairless Terrier
- **White or light-coated dogs**: Dalmatian, white Boxer, white Bull Terrier, white Pit mixes, Whippet, Bulldog
- **Thin-coated dogs**: Greyhound, Italian Greyhound
- **Dogs with light pigment**: pink noses, light eyelids, white ear tips
- **Any dog post-shave** or post-medical clip

The most-burned spots are the bridge of the nose, the ear tips, the belly (especially after shaving), the inner thighs, and the top of the head. White and pink-pigmented dogs can develop squamous cell carcinoma from chronic sun exposure, so this is not just a comfort issue.

### Dog-Safe Sunscreen Rules

Two ingredients to avoid in any sunscreen used on dogs:

- **Zinc oxide** is toxic to dogs if licked or ingested. Most human "sport" sunscreens contain it.
- **PABA** (para-aminobenzoic acid) is also toxic.

Look for fragrance-free, zinc-free, PABA-free sunscreen with SPF 15 or higher. Apply in shade. Wait 10 to 15 minutes before letting your dog free so it has time to absorb. The most commonly recommended pet brands are:

- **Epi-Pet K-9 Care Sunscreen** (the only FDA-compliant pet sunscreen sold in the US)
- **My Dog Nose It**
- **Petkin Doggy Sunmist**
- **Warren London Dog Sunscreen**

If you cannot find a pet product, a fragrance-free, zinc-free, PABA-free baby sunscreen is a fallback. Check with your vet first, especially for dogs that lick.

## PNW Summer Specifics

Pacific Northwest summer is short, intense, and often disrupted by smoke. The standard "Texas summer" advice does not all transfer.

### Wildfire Smoke (July to September)

Smoke season is now a regular feature of PNW summers. The AVMA flags these warning signs in dogs:

- Coughing, gagging, or difficulty breathing
- Eye watering, redness, or swelling
- Excessive nasal discharge
- Open-mouth breathing or wheezing
- Lethargy or weakness

When AQI hits unhealthy ranges, limit outdoor time and exercise. Run a HEPA filter at home. After any time outside in smoke, wipe down your dog's coat and paws because PM2.5 particles cling to fur. Rinse irritated eyes with lukewarm water on a cotton ball.

This is also why summer ear cleaning matters more than you might think. Smoke and fine ash settle into the ear canals of floppy-eared dogs, where they irritate skin and combine with moisture to cause infections.

### Lake and River Swimming

Lake season in the PNW is short but heavy. Three things to watch for:

- **Ear infections**. Water trapped in the ear canal becomes a yeast and bacteria incubator within 48 to 96 hours. Towel-dry ears after swims, lift floppy ears to let them air out, and use a vet-recommended drying ear cleaner if your dog swims regularly.
- **Hot spots on thick-coated dogs.** When wet undercoat does not dry fully, it cooks against the skin and creates raw, itchy patches. A pre-summer deshed and a thorough towel-dry after swims prevents this.
- **Blue-green algae**. Cyanobacteria blooms hit PNW lakes from July through September and are fatal to dogs. Do not let your dog drink from or swim in lake water that looks green, scummy, or has a paint-like sheen.

### Tick Season

The Western black-legged tick is active from spring through fall in the PNW. Do tick checks after every hike. Common hiding spots: ears, armpits, between toes, groin. Talk to your vet about year-round tick prevention if you hike with your dog regularly.

## Heat Prep Beyond the Groom

Summer dog care is not all about the haircut. Three things matter as much as the cut itself:

### The Hot Pavement Test

Place the back of your hand on the pavement and hold it there for 7 seconds. If you cannot, neither can your dog's paw pads.

The math:

- 77°F air = 125°F asphalt
- 86°F air = 135°F asphalt
- 90°F air = 140 to 160°F asphalt (burns paw pads in under 60 seconds)

Walk early morning or late evening on hot days. Stick to grass, dirt trails, or shaded sidewalks. For longer outings, consider booties (Ruffwear and Ultra Paws make solid options) or a paw wax like Musher's Secret as a supportive layer.

### Cooling Gear

A few categories worth knowing:

- **Evaporative cooling vests** soak in water and cool by evaporation. They work best in dry climates and are less effective on humid coastal days.
- **PCM or ice-based vests** like CoolerDog FlexiFreeze use phase-change material and work better in humid conditions.
- **Pressure-activated gel cooling mats** stay cool for about 3 hours without power. Good for indoor use, especially in PNW homes that lack AC.

### Avoid Mid-Day Grooming Pickups in Heatwaves

If you are scheduling a summer grooming appointment during a heatwave, ask for a morning drop-off and an early afternoon pickup. Avoid transporting your dog between 10 AM and 4 PM in a hot car. Many PNW salons run AC, but the trip to and from can be hotter than the salon itself.

<Callout>
Pro tip: Most PNW homes do not have central AC. Cooling mats and evening walks matter more here than they do in regions where homes are AC-equipped year-round. A $30 gel mat can save you a stressful summer week.
</Callout>

## What to Ask Your Groomer Before a Summer Appointment

Seven specific questions that get you the right summer groom:

1. **Will you deshed and trim my double-coated dog rather than shave?** A good groomer should be uncomfortable with shaving Huskies, Goldens, GSDs, or Aussies and should explain why.
2. **What blade length do you recommend** for my dog's coat type and our summer activity level (hiking, swimming)?
3. **Can you do a sanitary trim** (belly, paw pad fur, ear tips) even if we keep the body coat full?
4. **Do you use a high-velocity dryer** to fully dry the undercoat? (Prevents post-bath hot spots.)
5. **What time of day is best for pickup** so my dog is not transported in peak heat?
6. **Do you have AC and cooling mats** in the salon during heatwaves?
7. **If wildfire smoke hits, will you flush eyes and clean ears** as part of the bath?

A groomer who answers these comfortably is a groomer worth keeping. Our [guide to choosing a groomer](/blog/choosing-the-right-groomer-for-your-pet) has more of these screening questions.

## Bottom Line

For PNW summers specifically:

- **Double-coated dogs**: deshed every 4 to 8 weeks. Do not shave. Sanitary trims are fine.
- **Single-coated dogs**: a 1/2 to 1 inch summer cut keeps things cool. Keep the face and legs longer for shade and bug protection.
- **Light or thin-coated dogs**: pet-safe sunscreen, shade, and cooling gear matter more than the haircut.
- **Smoke season**: wipe coats and paws after outdoor time. Watch for eye and ear irritation. Limit outdoor exercise on bad-AQI days.
- **Lake season**: dry ears thoroughly. Avoid green or scummy water. Brush out wet undercoats to prevent hot spots.
