Winter dog grooming in the Pacific Northwest requires more frequent brushing (2 to 3 times per week minimum), longer coat lengths for insulation, and careful paw and skin care to handle months of rain, mud, and damp conditions. Here's how to keep your dog healthy and comfortable through the rainy season.

## Don't Skip the Groom

Regular grooming is more important in winter, not less. Damp, matted fur traps moisture against the skin, creating conditions for bacterial and fungal infections. According to [veterinary dermatologists](https://www.avma.org/), wet matted coats are one of the leading causes of winter hot spots in dogs. Keep to your regular grooming schedule, and if you're unsure how often that should be, check out our guide on [how often you should groom your dog](/blog/how-often-should-you-groom-your-dog).

Aim to brush your dog at least 2 to 3 times per week during the rainy months. This removes loose undercoat, distributes natural oils, and prevents mats from forming in damp fur. For double-coated breeds like Huskies, Samoyeds, and Australian Shepherds, you may need to brush even more frequently.

I see it every January: owners skip a grooming appointment because "it's too cold for a haircut." By February, they're bringing in a dog with pelted mats and irritated skin. Cold weather is not a reason to skip grooming. It's a reason to stay on schedule.

## Understanding PNW Winter Conditions

PNW winters are not like winters in the Midwest or Northeast. The cold here is different, and that matters for grooming.

Most of western Washington and Oregon sits in the 35 to 50 degree Fahrenheit range from November through March. Temperatures rarely drop below freezing for extended periods. What makes PNW winters hard on dogs isn't extreme cold. It's the relentless dampness.

We get rain 5 to 6 days a week for months at a time. The air stays humid. Dogs go outside and come back damp, then sit in a warm house with the heat running. The next day, they go out and get damp again. This wet-to-dry-to-wet cycle is harder on coats and skin than a steady deep freeze because:

- **Moisture never fully leaves the coat.** In dry-cold climates, fur dries quickly between outings. In the PNW, a dog's coat may never completely dry between walks.
- **Indoor heating creates extreme contrast.** Your dog goes from 40-degree rain to a 70-degree living room multiple times a day. That swing strips moisture from the skin and coat.
- **Mold and fungal spores thrive.** PNW winters are peak mold season. Damp coats pick up environmental allergens that dry climates don't deal with.

The takeaway: PNW winter grooming isn't about protecting your dog from blizzards. It's about managing a constant cycle of dampness, warmth, and dampness again. Our [seasonal grooming guide](/blog/pnw-seasonal-dog-grooming-guide) covers how to adjust your routine across all four PNW seasons.

## Temperature and Coat Management

A dog's coat is its primary defense against cold, so winter is not the time for close trims. But "keep the coat long" doesn't mean "let it grow unchecked."

### When to Trim vs. When to Keep Length

Ask your groomer for a "winter cut" that keeps the body coat 1 to 2 inches longer than a summer trim. At the same time, shorten areas prone to collecting moisture and mud: the belly, legs, paw pads, and sanitary area. This gives your dog insulation where it counts while keeping cleanup manageable.

For breeds like Poodles, Bichons, and Doodles, a moderate-length clip (1 to 1.5 inches on the body) balances warmth with maintenance. Going too long in a PNW winter just means more mats, because the coat stays damp longer and tangles faster.

### Double-Coated Breeds in PNW Winters

This comes up constantly, so I want to be clear: **never shave a double-coated dog.**

Breeds like Huskies, German Shepherds, Samoyeds, Golden Retrievers, and Australian Shepherds have a two-layer coat system. The dense undercoat insulates against both cold and heat. The longer guard hairs on top repel water and protect the skin.

Shaving this system does not help your dog stay cool in summer or dry faster in winter. It destroys the coat's natural insulation and water resistance. The undercoat often grows back patchy, soft, and less functional. In some cases, it never returns to normal.

What double-coated dogs DO need in winter:

- **Regular de-shedding treatments.** The undercoat still sheds in winter (less than spring, but it happens). Dead undercoat that isn't removed traps moisture and creates mats close to the skin.
- **Professional blowouts.** A groomer with a high-velocity dryer can blast out loose undercoat and fully dry the coat in ways a towel at home never will.
- **More frequent brushing.** Use an undercoat rake 2 to 3 times per week, followed by a slicker brush on the feathering (legs, chest, behind ears).

I groom several Samoyeds who hike year-round in the Washington Cascades. Their owners bring them in every 4 weeks through winter for a full de-shed and blowout. Those dogs never mat, never smell, and their coats stay healthy all season.

## Winter Paw Care

Paw care is the most overlooked part of winter dog grooming. The PNW doesn't use much road salt (unlike the Northeast and Midwest), so chemical burns on paw pads aren't a big concern here. But we have our own set of problems.

### Mud and Wet Trails

PNW dogs who hike, walk forest trails, or play in the yard deal with constant mud exposure from October through April. Mud packs between toes, hardens in paw pad fur, and creates a breeding ground for bacteria and yeast infections.

After every walk:

1. Rinse paws with lukewarm water (a shallow bin by the door works well)
2. Dry thoroughly between each toe
3. Trim any paw pad fur that's collecting debris

### Cracked Pads from Wet-Dry Cycles

Here's something most owners don't realize: the constant cycle of wet pavement and then warm, dry floors causes paw pads to crack. The pads absorb moisture outside, then lose it rapidly indoors. Over weeks, this creates dry, cracked, sometimes bleeding pads.

Prevention:

- Apply a paw balm or wax before walks to create a moisture barrier
- Reapply after drying paws when you get home
- Check pads weekly for cracks, splits, or rough texture
- Keep the fur between toes trimmed short so moisture doesn't sit against the skin

## Manage the Muddy Belly

A shorter belly trim makes a real difference in winter. Lower-slung dogs like Corgis, Dachshunds, and Basset Hounds are especially prone to dragging through mud and puddles. A clean belly clip combined with a longer body coat gives you the best of both worlds: warmth on top, easy cleanup underneath.

For more on handling mud and rain between grooming appointments, our [rain and mud grooming guide](/blog/rain-mud-fur-pnw-grooming-survival-guide) covers post-walk routines in detail.

## Drying Matters

Proper drying is essential for winter coat health. Never let your dog air-dry in cold weather. Use a towel or blow dryer on a low-heat setting after baths and wet walks. A thoroughly dried coat prevents bacterial growth, eliminates "wet dog smell," and reduces the risk of hot spots.

Pay extra attention to areas where moisture gets trapped: behind the ears, in the armpits, between the toes, and around the neck. These are the spots where bacterial and fungal infections are most likely to develop.

If your dog gets wet on walks daily (which is most PNW dogs from November to March), invest in a quality absorbent drying towel or a pet-specific blow dryer. Regular bath towels don't cut it for thick or double coats.

## Indoor Heating and Dry Skin

Dry skin is one of the most common winter grooming problems I see, and it's almost entirely caused by indoor heating.

PNW homes run heat from October through April. Forced air systems pull humidity out of the air. Your dog goes from damp outdoor conditions to a dry, heated house multiple times a day. The result: flaking, itching, dull coat, and sometimes persistent scratching that owners mistake for allergies.

### How to Prevent and Treat Winter Dry Skin

**Humidify your home.** Run a humidifier in the room where your dog sleeps. Target 40 to 50 percent humidity. This single step prevents most winter dry skin issues.

**Adjust your bathing products.** Switch to a moisturizing or oatmeal-based shampoo during winter months. Skip harsh degreasers unless your dog rolled in something genuinely foul. Follow every bath with a conditioning treatment.

**Don't over-bathe.** Every 4 to 6 weeks is usually enough for a full bath unless your dog is truly dirty. Over-bathing strips the natural oils that protect the skin. Between baths, use a waterless rinse-and-dry routine after muddy walks instead of full shampoos.

**Add omega fatty acids.** Fish oil supplements (omega-3 and omega-6) support skin health and coat quality from the inside out. You'll typically see results within 3 to 4 weeks of consistent supplementation. Check with your vet for the right dosage for your dog's size.

**Use a moisturizing spray.** Between baths, a light leave-in conditioning spray can help. Apply to the coat after brushing, focusing on areas that tend to get dry (back, flanks, belly). Don't soak the coat. A light mist is enough.

**Watch for warning signs.** If your dog is scratching constantly, has visible dandruff, or the skin looks red and inflamed, see your vet. Persistent dry skin can indicate an underlying condition that no amount of conditioner will fix.

## Trim Nails More Often

Dogs need nail trims every 2 to 3 weeks in winter, compared to every 3 to 4 weeks in summer. Softer, wetter ground doesn't wear nails down the way dry pavement does. Overgrown nails change your dog's gait and, according to the [American Kennel Club (AKC)](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/grooming/), can contribute to joint problems over time.

If your dog walks primarily on grass, trails, or soft ground through winter, you may need trims even more frequently. Listen for clicking on hard floors. That's your cue.

## Winter Grooming Schedule Adjustments

Winter doesn't mean you groom less. For most dogs, winter grooming frequency should stay the same or increase slightly.

**Professional grooming:** Stick to your regular schedule (every 4 to 8 weeks depending on breed). If anything, book slightly more often in winter because damp conditions accelerate matting.

**Brushing:** Increase from your baseline frequency. If you brush twice a week in summer, bump to 3 to 4 times in winter. The extra dampness in PNW conditions means tangles form faster.

**Bathing:** Keep the same frequency (every 4 to 6 weeks for most dogs), but add more rinsing-and-drying routines between baths after wet walks. The goal is removing mud and moisture without stripping oils.

**Nail trims:** Move from every 3 to 4 weeks to every 2 to 3 weeks.

**Ear cleaning:** Check ears weekly in winter. Breeds with floppy ears (Cocker Spaniels, Goldens, Doodles) are especially prone to ear infections when moisture gets trapped. A quick wipe with an ear cleaning solution after wet outings can prevent problems.

## When to See Your Groomer

Even with solid at-home care, professional grooming makes a big difference in winter. A groomer can handle de-shedding treatments, express anal glands, check for skin issues hiding under a thick coat, and give your dog a proper warm bath with professional-grade drying equipment.

In my salon, I always tell winter clients: if something looks or smells off, don't wait for your next appointment. Bring the dog in. Skin issues that develop under a thick winter coat can go from minor irritation to a serious infection in a week or two.

## FAQ

**Should you groom your dog in winter?**
Yes, winter grooming is essential. Damp, matted fur traps moisture against the skin and can cause bacterial infections, hot spots, and discomfort. Brush at least 2 to 3 times per week and maintain your regular professional grooming schedule (every 4 to 8 weeks).

**How often should you brush your dog in winter?**
Most dogs benefit from brushing 3 to 4 times per week in winter, up from 2 to 3 times in drier months. Double-coated breeds and dogs that spend a lot of time outdoors may need daily brushing to prevent matting from moisture.

**Should you cut your dog's hair short in winter?**
No. Keep the coat 1 to 2 inches longer than a summer cut for insulation against cold and rain. Ask your groomer for a "winter cut" that maintains warmth on the body while trimming mud-prone areas like the belly, legs, and paws. Never shave a double-coated breed.

**How do you prevent matting in wet weather?**
Brush regularly, dry your dog thoroughly after rain exposure, and keep up with professional grooming appointments. Mats form faster in damp conditions, so don't let weeks go by without brushing. Brush while the coat is still slightly damp (before it dries and sets into tangles).

**Why is my dog's skin so dry and flaky in winter?**
Indoor heating is the most common cause. Forced air systems strip humidity from the air, drying out your dog's skin. Run a humidifier (target 40 to 50 percent humidity), use moisturizing shampoo, avoid over-bathing, and consider adding an omega fatty acid supplement to their diet. If flaking persists or the skin looks red and irritated, consult your vet.

**Can you shave a double-coated dog in winter?**
No. Double-coated breeds (Huskies, Samoyeds, German Shepherds, Golden Retrievers, Australian Shepherds) rely on their two-layer coat system for insulation and water resistance. Shaving removes this protection and the undercoat often grows back damaged. Instead, keep up with regular de-shedding treatments and professional blowouts.
