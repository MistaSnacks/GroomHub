Winter grooming should keep your dog's coat manageable, their skin comfortable, and their paws free of irritating debris. Continue needed brushing, nail care, and professional maintenance, but choose haircut length and bathing frequency for the individual dog. A longer coat is useful only when you can keep it clean and free of mats.

## Plan for the Weather Your Dog Actually Encounters

Western lowlands, mountain routes, and communities east of the Cascades do not share one winter climate. Think about your dog's actual exposure: wet neighborhood walks, snow trips, salted sidewalks, or long periods indoors. Check current conditions before outings rather than relying on a regional temperature average.

Build the grooming plan around those activities. A dog that repeatedly collects debris around the feet may need a different tidy from a short-coated dog whose main problem is staying comfortable in cold rain.

## Should You Cut a Dog's Hair in Winter?

Ask for an appropriate maintenance cut, not an undefined “winter cut.” Discuss body length, hygiene areas, and the brushing you can maintain. Do not request an extra inch or two everywhere without considering coat type and matting.

[ASPCA's cold-weather guidance](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/general-pet-care/cold-weather-safety-tips) advises retaining protection from the cold and cleaning and drying feet after exposure to ice and chemicals. If close clipping is needed because of matting or medical care, ask the groomer and veterinarian about protection afterward. Routine cosmetic shaving and necessary treatment are different decisions.

Double-coated dogs usually need suitable brushing and loose-coat removal rather than a routine short body cut. Do not let a blanket “never shave” rule prevent an assessment of a painful coat. Our [matted-dog guide](/blog/severely-matted-dog-grooming-guide) explains the relevant questions.

## Brush According to Coat Condition

Check the collar area, armpits, belly, leg feathering, and behind the ears. Ask the groomer to demonstrate a light technique that reaches through the coat without scraping the skin. Repeated resistance from the comb means reassessing the area, not brushing harder.

A dog's winter schedule is not determined by rain alone. Keep a note of where tangles return and discuss adjusting the haircut, tools, or appointment interval. See the [grooming-frequency guide](/blog/how-often-should-you-groom-your-dog) for a way to separate daily maintenance from salon visits.

## Clean and Check Paws After Outings

Do not assume that the Pacific Northwest has no de-icing chemicals. Exposure depends on the route, weather, and property. Check paws and the underside, rinse off dirt or residue, and dry gently. Look for redness, cracks, or anything lodged between toes.

Discuss paw protection with your veterinarian if your dog repeatedly becomes sore. Booties must fit and allow normal movement; balms are not a treatment for an injury. Leave trimming between pads to someone who can do it safely.

If a dog is limping, persistently licking one paw, or reacting painfully to touch, contact a veterinarian. A cosmetic paw tidy should not delay an examination.

## Drying and Bathing in Cold Weather

Use an absorbent towel and a comfortable indoor area after wet outings. A thick coat may require more drying support than a quick surface wipe. Ask a groomer to show an appropriate method if you struggle to dry the coat or your dog dislikes the equipment.

Do not use high heat, leave a dog unattended with a dryer, or force them to tolerate distress. For ordinary mud, targeted rinsing may avoid an unnecessary full shampoo. Follow product directions and any veterinary bathing plan. Our [rain and mud guide](/blog/rain-mud-fur-pnw-grooming-survival-guide) gives a practical cleanup sequence.

## Dry Skin Is a Symptom, Not a Diagnosis

Flaking or itching can have several causes. Do not assume indoor heating explains it, or that a humidifier, oatmeal shampoo, or fish-oil supplement will fix it. Note when the problem started and whether there are sore areas, hair loss, odor, or persistent scratching.

Ask your veterinarian about recurring symptoms before changing products or adding supplements. A groomer can report an unusual area but cannot diagnose the cause. The [AAHA senior-pet guidance](https://www.aaha.org/resources/aging-with-grace-how-to-support-your-senior-pet/) is particularly useful when an older dog's grooming habits or behavior have changed.

## Keep Nail Care Separate From Haircuts

Check nails between appointments, including dewclaws. A change in activity or walking surface can affect wear, but there is no universal winter-only trimming interval. Ask a groomer or veterinarian to help assess the right schedule and read our [nail-trim guide](/blog/dog-nail-trimming-cost-guide) for service options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I skip winter grooming so my dog stays warm?

Keep necessary coat and hygiene care. Discuss a practical length and appropriate protection rather than allowing mats to build beneath a long coat.

### How often should I bathe my dog in winter?

Use actual dirt, coat condition, and veterinary instructions as the guide. Paw rinsing after a walk is different from a full shampoo, and frequent washing is not a solution for unexplained itching.

### Do all dogs need a winter haircut?

No. Some need coat maintenance without a body haircut. Others benefit from an agreed trim that can be maintained comfortably at home.
